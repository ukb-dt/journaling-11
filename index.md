# [00](https://eplnm.github.io/1st/)
Energy can be expressed in terms of **wavelength** using the **Planck–Einstein relation**, which applies to electromagnetic radiation (like light).

### Relationship between energy and wavelength

$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

Where:

* $E$ = energy (in joules, J)
* $h$ = Planck’s constant $= 6.626 \times 10^{-34},\text{J·s}$
* $c$ = speed of light $= 3.00 \times 10^{8},\text{m/s}$
* $\lambda$ = wavelength (in meters)

### Key idea

* **Shorter wavelength → higher energy**
* **Longer wavelength → lower energy**

### Common forms

* In joules:
  $$
  E = \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^{8})}{\lambda}
  $$

* In electronvolts (eV), for convenience in physics/chemistry:
  $$
  E(\text{eV}) = \frac{1240}{\lambda(\text{nm})}
  $$

### Example

If light has a wavelength of **500 nm**:
$$
E = \frac{1240}{500} = 2.48\ \text{eV}
$$

# [01](https://ukb-dt.github.io/journaling-10/)
Yes — **for electromagnetic waves in a vacuum**, that is exactly right.

$$
\nu \lambda = c
$$

Where:

* $\nu$ (nu) = frequency (Hz)
* $\lambda$ = wavelength (m)
* $c$ = speed of light $= 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$

### Why this works

Wave speed is given by:
$$
\text{speed} = \text{frequency} \times \text{wavelength}
$$
For light in a vacuum, the speed is always (c), so:
$$
c = \nu \lambda
$$

### Important notes

* In a **medium** (like water or glass), the speed is lower:
  $$
  v = \nu \lambda \quad \text{with } v < c
  $$
* The **frequency stays the same** when light enters a medium, but the **wavelength changes**.

### Connection to energy

Combining with $E = h\nu$:
$$
E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

So your understanding is spot on 👍

**Hertz (Hz) is equivalent to inverse seconds:**

$$
1\ \text{Hz} = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}
$$

### Why this makes sense

* Frequency = number of cycles **per second**
* “Per second” mathematically means **divided by seconds**, i.e. (1/s)

So:

* $10\ \text{Hz} = 10\ \text{cycles per second} = 10\ \text{s}^{-1}$
* $nu$ (frequency) always has units of $\text{s}^{-1}$

### Unit check (example)

For $c = \nu \lambda$:

$$
(\text{s}^{-1}) \times (\text{m}) = \text{m/s}
$$

which matches the units of speed perfectly.
